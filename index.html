The problem

Passwords are essential to staying secure online, but a strong one is usually a wall of random characters and security jargon nobody actually enjoys remembering.

A passphrase fixes both problems at once: a few ordinary words, tempered with a couple of substitutions, are far easier to recall than a random string, and because of their length, often just as hard, or harder, to crack.

What "tempering" does

Start with words you can actually recall. That's the part your memory does. Then we heat-treat them: swap some letters for look-alike numbers and symbols (a → @, e → 3, i → 1, o → 0, s → $), and randomly recase letters. The result is much harder to guess than the plain phrase, while staying close enough to what you typed that you can still reconstruct it.

Four or five unrelated words tempered this way usually lands in "fortified" territory, and it's far easier to remember than a random string of the same length.

Why words beat characters

Length beats complexity. Every word you add multiplies the number of possible passphrases by 2,048, and a long phrase is far easier to remember than a short jumble of symbols. Four unrelated words already match an 8-character password with every character type switched on.

We measure strength in bits of entropy: words × log₂(2,048) = words × 11. Each bit doubles the number of guesses an attacker needs. The words must be picked at random: a phrase you invent yourself is much easier to guess.

These crack times show the worst case: an attacker has stolen your password's hash and is guessing offline on fast modern hardware. It's a realistic estimate, not a scare tactic.
